Cybersecurity Essentials: Working Securely from Anywhere

An interactive workplace learning concept designed to help employees recognize common cybersecurity threats, make safer decisions, and apply practical security habits in everyday work situations.

Instructional Design · Scenario-Based Learning · eLearning
01 / Project Overview

Making cybersecurity easier to understand and apply

Employees regularly handle sensitive information across different environments, including offices, homes, and public spaces. Understanding cybersecurity policies is important, but employees also need to recognize risks and know how to respond when situations arise.

The learning challenge

How can employees practise recognizing cybersecurity risks and responding confidently to suspicious situations without relying solely on lengthy policy documents?

Target Audience

Employees who use digital tools, communicate online, and access organizational information in office, remote, or hybrid work settings.

Learning Format

A short, self-paced digital learning experience designed to take approximately 15–20 minutes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the experience, learners should be able to:

  • Recognize common phishing and social engineering indicators.
  • Choose safer responses to realistic workplace situations.
  • Apply practical habits to protect company information.

Tools & Methods

Scenario writing, interactive knowledge checks, instructional feedback, and a responsive browser-based learning prototype.

02 / Instructional Design Strategy

Designing for practical application

Rather than presenting cybersecurity information as a list of rules, the learning experience uses familiar workplace situations to help learners recognize risks, consider their options, and practise making informed decisions.

01

Authentic workplace scenarios

Situations involving suspicious emails and shared workspaces connect cybersecurity concepts to decisions employees may encounter in their daily work.

02

Decision-based activities

Learners select how they would respond to realistic situations. This encourages active participation and application rather than passive reading.

03

Immediate instructional feedback

Feedback explains why an answer is appropriate or risky, reinforcing correct decisions and helping address misconceptions.

04

Knowledge checks and retrieval practice

Short questions give learners opportunities to recall and apply key concepts throughout the experience.

05

Practical transfer to the workplace

A final checklist and personal commitment prompt encourage learners to identify habits they can apply beyond the course.

WELCOME

Working securely starts with you

Cybersecurity is not just the responsibility of IT teams. Every employee plays a role in protecting company information, accounts, and digital systems.

Whether you are working at the office, from home, or in a public space, everyday decisions can affect the security of the information you handle.

Your approach: Pause. Verify. Protect.

Take a moment before acting. Verify unexpected requests. Protect information by following approved security practices.

What you will learn

  • Identify warning signs in suspicious messages.
  • Recognize common cybersecurity risks.
  • Choose safer responses to workplace situations.
  • Build everyday habits that protect information.

Take your time. You will receive feedback as you work through the activities.

UNDERSTANDING THE RISKS

Where can security risks happen?

Cybersecurity threats can appear in everyday activities. Understanding common risks is the first step toward preventing them.

Phishing & Social Engineering

Messages designed to trick people into sharing sensitive information or taking unsafe actions.

Suspicious Links & Attachments

Unexpected links or files can lead to fraudulent websites or malicious downloads.

Weak Account Security

Reused or easy-to-guess passwords can make accounts vulnerable to unauthorized access.

Unsecured Networks

Public or untrusted networks may expose sensitive information if approved security measures are not used.

Information Exposure

Documents, screens, and conversations can unintentionally expose confidential information.

Unexpected Requests

Someone may impersonate a colleague, manager, or service provider to create urgency and bypass normal checks.

Remember

A message that looks familiar is not necessarily legitimate. Always verify unexpected requests before taking action.

SCENARIO 01 · PHISHING

A message that needs your attention

Sarah is working remotely when she receives an email claiming that her company account will be suspended unless she verifies her information within 30 minutes.

From: support@northstarr-secure.com
Subject: URGENT: Your account will be suspended

Hello Sarah,

We detected an issue with your account. Your access will be suspended in 30 minutes unless you verify your credentials immediately.

Please use the link below to confirm your account:

northstar-account.help/login

Thank you,
Northstar IT Support

Can you spot the warning signs?

Look carefully at the message. Consider these indicators:

  • Look-alike sender: The email domain is slightly different from the expected company domain.
  • Manufactured urgency: The 30-minute deadline pressures Sarah to act quickly.
  • Credential request: The message asks Sarah to provide her login information.
  • Suspicious destination: The link does not match an expected company website.
Think before you click.

Urgent language, unexpected requests for credentials, and unfamiliar links are reasons to stop and verify.

SCENARIO 01 · YOUR DECISION

What should Sarah do?

Sarah needs to decide how to respond to the suspicious email. What is the safest course of action?

Key takeaway

Never use contact details or links from a suspicious message to verify that same message. Use a known, trusted channel.

SCENARIO 02 · REMOTE WORK

Protecting information in a shared space

Jordan is working from a coffee shop while waiting for a meeting to begin. He joins a video call to discuss a confidential project document.

The situation

People are sitting nearby, the Wi-Fi network is public, and Jordan has opened a document containing sensitive business information.

What should Jordan do?

Key takeaway

Consider your surroundings before accessing or discussing confidential information. Protect screens, conversations, and network connections.

04 / Assessment & Evaluation

How learning could be evaluated

The prototype includes formative knowledge checks and scenario-based decision-making activities. In a workplace implementation, additional evaluation could help determine whether the learning translates into safer everyday behaviours.

Knowledge Assessment

Review quiz scores and scenario responses to identify whether learners can recognize common security risks and select appropriate actions.

Learner Feedback

Collect learner feedback about the clarity, relevance, accessibility, and usefulness of the learning experience.

Confidence & Application

Use short pre- and post-learning confidence questions to explore whether learners feel more prepared to respond to suspicious workplace situations.

Workplace Indicators

Where appropriate, review longer-term indicators such as reporting behaviour, phishing simulation results, and recurring security awareness gaps.

Evaluation note

These are proposed evaluation methods for the concept. No organizational pilot, learner study, or measured performance improvement is being claimed.

05 / Design Reflection

Bringing UX and learning design together

This project explores how user experience principles can support effective digital learning. Clear navigation, consistent layouts, accessible interactions, and meaningful feedback help learners focus on the content and understand what to do next.

The design process involved breaking down cybersecurity concepts into manageable topics, writing realistic scenarios, and creating opportunities for learners to practise decision-making.

It also reinforced the importance of connecting learning objectives, instructional activities, and assessment. A successful learning experience should do more than present information; it should help people apply what they have learned in practical situations.
