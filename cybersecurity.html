The design challenge

Helping employees recognize risks before they act.

Cybersecurity awareness is not just about knowing the rules. Employees need to recognize warning signs and make appropriate decisions when faced with unexpected messages, requests, and situations.

How might we help employees turn cybersecurity knowledge into safer everyday decisions?
Project scope

An independent instructional design concept for a fictional organization. The course focuses on phishing, social engineering, and secure remote-working habits.

Remote and hybrid work introduce situations where employees may receive unexpected requests, access information outside the office, or work in environments with less direct support.

A traditional information-heavy approach can explain cybersecurity policies, but it may not give learners enough opportunity to practise applying those policies in realistic situations.

For this concept, I chose a short scenario-based format. Learners encounter a suspicious email, identify indicators of risk, and practise deciding what to do next. The goal is to connect information with practical decision-making.

The course uses a recurring fictional employee, Sarah, to provide continuity and give learners a recognizable workplace context.

Audience and learning need

Designed for employees who need practical guidance.

Intended audience

Employees in office, hybrid, and remote work environments who use email, collaboration platforms, shared documents, and company systems. The concept assumes learners have basic familiarity with everyday workplace technology.

Learning challenge

Learners may recognize the term phishing but still struggle to evaluate an unexpected message, resist pressure, or know how to respond after interacting with a suspicious link.

Potential barriers

  • Urgent messages can encourage rushed decisions.
  • Fraudulent messages can imitate familiar organizations.
  • Employees may be unsure how to report suspicious activity.
  • Security guidance can feel abstract without examples.

Design response

  • Use realistic workplace scenarios.
  • Highlight observable warning signs.
  • Provide decision-making opportunities.
  • Explain why each response is safer or riskier.

Instructional strategy

From information to decision-making.

The instructional approach combines concise explanations with opportunities to practise. Learners are asked to make decisions, receive feedback, and consider how the guidance applies to their work.

01

Pause

Stop before responding to unexpected messages, urgent requests, unfamiliar links, or requests for sensitive information.

02

Verify

Check the sender, confirm the request through a trusted channel, and look for warning signs before taking action.

03

Protect

Avoid interacting with suspicious content, report concerns using approved workplace procedures, and take appropriate next steps.

Section 01 · Introduction

Working securely starts with everyday decisions.

Think about how often you receive emails, open shared documents, access company systems, or respond to requests from colleagues. Each interaction is an opportunity to protect information.

Why this matters

Cybersecurity is not only the responsibility of IT teams. Employees play an important role by recognizing unusual activity, protecting information, and reporting concerns.

Consider this

You receive an unexpected message asking you to confirm your account details immediately. What would you normally do first? Keep your answer in mind as you work through the course.

Section 02 · Recognize threats

Not every message is what it seems.

Phishing is a type of social engineering that uses deceptive messages to persuade people to reveal information, open harmful attachments, or visit fraudulent websites.

Unexpected requests

A message asks you to provide information or complete an action you were not expecting.

Urgent language

Threats of account suspension, missed payments, or immediate consequences pressure you to act quickly.

Unfamiliar sender

The display name may look familiar, but the actual email address or domain may be different.

Suspicious links

A link may lead to a website designed to imitate a legitimate login page.

Requests for sensitive details

A message asks for passwords, verification codes, payment details, or confidential information.

Unexpected attachments

An unfamiliar file arrives without a clear business reason or expected context.

Example · Suspicious message Review carefully

From: IT Support <support@northstar-secure.example>

Subject: Immediate action required

Hello,

Your company account will be suspended today unless you verify your login credentials immediately.

Please use the link below to restore access.

Verify your account now

Look for: an unexpected request, urgency, and a request for login details.

Remember

One warning sign does not always prove a message is fraudulent. Consider the whole message and verify unexpected requests through a trusted channel.

Key learning point

A message can appear professional and still be fraudulent. Verify unexpected requests independently rather than using contact details or links supplied in the suspicious message.

Section 04 · Practical framework

Use Pause–Verify–Protect.

When a message or request feels unusual, use this simple sequence before taking action.

01

Pause

Do not rush. Avoid clicking unexpected links, opening unfamiliar attachments, or sharing information under pressure.

02

Verify

Check the sender and request. Contact the person or department using a trusted phone number, known website, or established channel.

03

Protect

Follow your organization's reporting procedure. If you already interacted with the message, report it promptly and follow IT or security guidance.

Put the framework into practice

You receive an unexpected message from a senior leader asking you to purchase gift cards and send the codes immediately.

Pause: Do not purchase anything under pressure.

Verify: Contact the person through a known, independent communication channel.

Protect: Report the suspicious request according to your organization's procedures.

Important

Reporting channels and incident procedures vary by organization. Always follow your employer's approved cybersecurity policies.

Section 05 · Workplace practice

Choose a safer response.

Apply what you have learned to three common remote-working situations. Select an answer and read the feedback before moving on.

Scenario 1 · Password security

You need to remember several work passwords.

A colleague suggests saving them in a document on your desktop.

Scenario 2 · Public spaces

You are working at an airport.

You need to review a document containing confidential company information.

Scenario 3 · Incident response

You accidentally clicked a suspicious link.

You are worried that you may have exposed your account details.

Why practice matters

Decision-making practice helps learners connect guidance with the kinds of situations they may encounter during everyday work.

Assessment and evaluation

How would I know whether the learning worked?

Completing a course is not the same as demonstrating learning. This concept uses a combination of knowledge checks, learner feedback, and potential workplace indicators to evaluate effectiveness.

Knowledge and understanding

Use scenario questions and knowledge checks to assess whether learners can identify phishing indicators and select appropriate responses.

Confidence and usability

Gather learner feedback about clarity, relevance, accessibility, and confidence in responding to suspicious messages.

Workplace application

Where appropriate, review aggregated reporting trends and security awareness indicators over time. Interpret changes alongside other organizational factors.

Potential evaluation measures

Knowledge-check performance, scenario decision accuracy, learner feedback, course completion, and trends in reported suspicious messages. These are proposed measures for a future implementation, not results from this portfolio prototype.

Design reflection

What this project demonstrates.

The goal is not simply to teach employees what phishing is. It is to help them recognize it and know what to do next.

This project explores how a practical workplace topic can be transformed into a short learning experience with clear objectives, realistic examples, guided practice, and assessment.

The use of a recurring scenario gives learners an opportunity to apply information in context. The Pause–Verify–Protect framework provides a memorable structure they can recall when faced with unexpected requests.

A future version could be developed in an authoring tool such as Articulate Rise, with additional accessibility testing, narration options, and an LMS-compatible course package.

The next design iteration would include feedback from representative learners and subject-matter experts to validate the examples, procedures, and technical accuracy.
